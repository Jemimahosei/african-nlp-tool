In plain language: most of the world’s languages are African, but almost none of them work well with modern AI. The African Languages Lab (All Lab) spent years collecting text and speech in 40 African languages, built a community platform so speakers themselves could contribute and check translations, and then used that data to teach a language model to translate English into those languages — far better than the model could before. This site lets you explore the dataset, the results, and try a small piece of the idea yourself.

Every number on this page comes from the paper itself (Issaka et al., 2025) — Tables 1–3 and the abstract — and is cited where it appears. Nothing here is invented. The live model is not connected yet; the demo box below is explicit about what is real and what is a stand-in.

01 The problem

Of the roughly 7,000 languages spoken in the world, fewer than 20 get serious attention from NLP research. Africa alone has more than 2,000 languages — nearly a third of all languages on Earth — yet 88% of African languages are “severely underrepresented” or “completely ignored” in computational linguistics (Joshi et al., 2020). About 814 African languages are in danger of extinction. Nigeria, Cameroon and Ivory Coast have the most languages at severe risk (171, 75 and 65 respectively).

The research gap is measurable. Counting papers about the top 10 global languages versus the top 10 African languages (2020–2024), the paper finds a 20–70× underrepresentation:

That gap is not abstract. When a language has no usable data, it gets no working translation, no voice tools, no spell-check — and its speakers are shut out of education, healthcare information and the digital economy in their own language.

02 What the lab did

Founded in 2020, the African Languages Lab attacks the gap on three fronts at once: collect the data, build the models, and train the people.

1 · All Voices

A mobile-first, community-driven data collection platform — the only one designed specifically for low-resource languages. Speakers translate directly between African languages without English as an intermediary. Translations are peer-reviewed: a translation only counts as verified after more than 5 upvotes and fewer than 3 downvotes from the community. Verified translations then become source material for further translations, so the data multiplies. Built with React Native and Firebase; contributions are consent-based and revocable, protected with AES-256 encryption at rest and TLS 1.3 in transit.

2 · The dataset

Community translations from All Voices, combined with curated open corpora (NLLB, CCMatrix, ParaCrawl, OpenSubtitles and others), cleaned with language-specific processing — tone-mark standardisation, script-variant normalisation, morphological analysis — and validated with a statistical framework that samples 10,000 translation pairs per language pair. The result is the largest validated African multi-modal speech and text dataset: 19 billion tokens across 40 languages, plus 12,628 hours of aligned speech.

3 · Models & mentorship

The team fine-tuned Llama-3.2-1B — a model that had never been trained on any of these African languages, giving a clean baseline — on the new data, and evaluated it on the FLORES-200 benchmark across 31 languages. In parallel, a structured programme mentored fifteen early-career researchers across four institutions, building local capacity to keep the work going.

How the experiment worked

  • Base model: Llama-3.2-1B. Chosen because it can tokenise these languages but was never trained on them — so any improvement is attributable to the lab’s data, not to memorisation.
  • Training: full fine-tuning (parameter-efficient QLoRA was tried first and did not gain enough), one epoch, instruction template “Translate the following English text to X:”, NVIDIA H100 GPUs, learning rate 5×10−5.
  • Test set: FLORES-200, the standard multilingual benchmark — the same sentences in every language, so results are comparable.
  • Metrics, in plain words: ChrF++ compares character sequences, which matters for languages where one word can carry a lot of meaning; BLEU counts matching word sequences; COMET is a neural score for whether the meaning survived; AfriCOMET is COMET retrained for African language pairs; METEOR credits word stems and synonyms; TER counts the edits a human would need to fix the translation — lower is better.

03 The dataset — 40 languages, two divides

The data is not evenly spread, and the paper is honest about that. A few languages hold most of the text; a different few hold most of the audio. Amharic alone has 2.94 billion tokens; Fang has 0.02 million — a 147,000× disparity. Languages with over 1 billion tokens average ChrF++ 45.66 after fine-tuning; those under 100 million average 24.31. The imbalance is part of the finding, not a footnote.

04 Model vs baseline — the results

Fine-tuning on the lab’s data improved the base model on every headline metric, averaged across the 31 evaluated languages:

Average gains over baseline: +23.69 ChrF++, +0.33 COMET, +15.34 BLEU. (Abstract; Table 3 averages: ChrF++ 8.10 → 31.79, COMET 0.32 → 0.65, BLEU 2.27 → 17.61.) Translation edit effort fell by an average of 580.13 TER points — Swahili’s fine-tuned TER of 23.77 exactly matches Google Translate, meaning its output needs about as little post-editing as Google’s.

What stands out

  • Swahili gained the most: ChrF++ 9.00 → 72.27 (+63.27), nearly matching Google Translate’s 75.81. Its BLEU jumped 0.54 → 56.23 — above Google’s 54.81.
  • The fine-tuned model beats Google Translate on ChrF++ in Twi (46.80 vs 31.48), Yoruba (30.88 vs 21.05), Arabic (31.52 vs 28.46), Sesotho (61.79 vs 57.01), Luganda (24.91 vs 23.55) and Tigrinya (24.12 vs 23.75).
  • Where Google still leads: high-resource cases like Kinyarwanda (24.65 vs 70.27) and Zulu (38.06 vs 64.45) — and in 9 languages Google offers no support at all, which is exactly the gap this work fills.
  • Not every language improved: Fon’s ChrF++ fell slightly (10.57 → 9.47, −1.10), and Fon, Wolof and Bambara improved least — the paper flags morphologically complex languages as needing specialised tokenisation or architectures.
  • Character gains aren’t always meaning gains: Ewe improved +30.43 ChrF++ but only +0.04 COMET, while Arabic gained +0.33 COMET on a modest ChrF++ rise — which is why the paper reports six metrics, not one.

07 Limits & ethics, honestly

The paper states its own limits, and this demo repeats them rather than hiding them:

  • Only one base model was tried (Llama-3.2-1B); larger architectures might do better, and the best model probably varies by language family.
  • Only 31 of the 40 collected languages were evaluated — FLORES-200 does not cover the other nine, which are among the most under-resourced.
  • 13 languages have no audio data at all, and validation ran without native-speaker verification for 73% of languages.
  • Data collection is consent-based and revocable through All Voices, with community reporting for offensive content; some dataset components are under managed access out of respect for contributing communities.
  • The work is guided by Ubuntu philosophy — inclusivity, interdependence and openness — and framed as a contribution to the UN Sustainable Development Goals on education and inequality.
