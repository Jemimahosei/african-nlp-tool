The African Languages Lab

Africa holds nearly a third of the world's languages, and almost none of them work well with AI. The lab gathers text and speech with the people who speak them, trains translation models on it, and mentors the young researchers doing the work.

Presented at ACL 2026 in San Diego. Twenty authors, including Jemimah Osei of Cornell.

Most of these languages have almost no data for AI to learn from

Of about 7,000 languages worldwide, fewer than 20 get serious attention from language technology researchers. Africa has more than 2,000 of them. Of those, 88 percent are severely underrepresented or completely ignored in computational linguistics, and about 814 are in danger of dying out.

The research record shows the same gap. For every paper on African languages in multilingual models, there are 20 to 70 papers on the world's top languages, depending on the database.

What the lab does about it

All Voices

A mobile app where speakers translate directly between African languages, with no English in the middle. A translation counts as verified after more than 5 upvotes and fewer than 3 downvotes from the community. Contributions are consent based and can be withdrawn.

The dataset

Community translations plus open corpora, cleaned for tone marks, scripts and word structure. The result is 19 billion tokens across 40 languages, with 12,628 hours of aligned speech.

What stands out

  • Swahili reached 72.27 ChrF++, up from 9.00 for the base model and close to Google Translate at 75.81.
  • On ChrF++ the model beats Google Translate in Twi, Yoruba, Arabic, Sesotho, Luganda and Tigrinya.
  • Not every language improved. Fon fell slightly on ChrF++, and the score view above flags every language that did worse than the base model.
  • Character matches and meaning scores can disagree. Ewe gained 30.43 ChrF++ points but only 0.04 on COMET, which is why the paper reports six scores.

How the experiment worked

  • Base model: Llama-3.2-1B. Chosen because it can tokenise these languages but was never trained on them — so any improvement is attributable to the lab’s data, not to memorisation.
  • Training: full fine-tuning (parameter-efficient QLoRA was tried first and did not gain enough), one epoch, instruction template “Translate the following English text to X:”, NVIDIA H100 GPUs, learning rate 5×10−5.
  • Test set: FLORES-200, the standard multilingual benchmark — the same sentences in every language, so results are comparable.
  • Metrics, in plain words: ChrF++ compares character sequences, which matters for languages where one word can carry a lot of meaning; BLEU counts matching word sequences; COMET is a neural score for whether the meaning survived; AfriCOMET is COMET retrained for African language pairs; METEOR credits word stems and synonyms; TER counts the edits a human would need to fix the translation — lower is better.
